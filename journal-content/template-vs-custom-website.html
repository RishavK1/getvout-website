Choose a template or a custom website by testing how well it supports your content, editing workflow and integrations. Neither approach guarantees good SEO, fast performance or a distinctive brand. The implementation and the fit matter more than the label.

When is a template a good choice?

A template can be appropriate when the content structure is conventional, the integrations are limited and the editing model suits the people maintaining the site. A small team may benefit more from familiar publishing tools than from a completely bespoke interface it cannot confidently update.

Test that assumption with real tasks. Ask the intended editor to add a service, change a team profile, replace an image and publish an announcement. Note where they need help, what cannot be changed safely and which adjustments require a developer.

When does custom development make sense?

Custom work becomes useful when important requirements repeatedly fight the available structure: a specialized user journey, connected internal systems, unusual content relationships or a carefully controlled interaction model. The case is strongest when you can identify the limitation and explain its practical cost.

“We want something different” is a starting conversation, not a complete specification. Decide which differences help your audience. A custom build still needs sensible editing, testing, deployment and support arrangements; writing more code does not remove those responsibilities.

Is a custom website better for SEO?

Not automatically. Google’s guidance emphasizes useful content and technical fundamentals, not a preference for a particular authoring tool. Both approaches can produce crawlable pages with descriptive titles, readable content and accurate structured data. Both can also produce confusing navigation or unnecessary scripts.

Compare representative pages instead of making platform-wide assumptions. Use the same content and images where practical. Inspect loading, responsiveness, keyboard use, headings, URLs and what the page delivers before JavaScript runs. Treat a single laboratory score as one signal, not a prediction of search rankings.

What should a proposal compare?

  • Editing: who can change what, and with how much training?
  • Integrations: which systems connect, and who maintains them?
  • Ownership: what happens to content, domains and access when suppliers change?
  • Maintenance: which updates and checks are included after launch?
  • Constraints: which requirements are deliberately outside the initial scope?

How do we make the decision?

Start with a short requirements discussion, then compare the smallest realistic implementations. A template that handles the important tasks may be the right recommendation. A custom system may be justified when those tasks require a different structure. The answer should be understandable without a sales pitch against either category.

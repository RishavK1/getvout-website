For a high-risk action, the interface should help a person understand the consequence, review the important information and correct or reverse a mistake where appropriate. A more dramatic button is not the same thing as a safer transaction.

What makes a confirmation useful?

Imagine a fictional training account with $125 in interest and a $2,500 principal balance. The intended task is to pay the interest, not close the account. A technically accurate form can still be hard to use if its labels describe internal bookkeeping rather than that intention. The demonstration above uses invented figures; no payment is sent.

Before the final action, show the amount, the recipient or account, and whether the action pays interest or settles the full balance. These facts belong near the confirmation, not only in an earlier screen. A reviewer should not need to reconstruct the result from several unrelated settings.

What does WCAG say about error prevention?

WCAG 2.2 criterion 3.3.4 covers specified legal, financial and data-changing submissions. It describes alternatives: the submission can be reversible, entered data can be checked with a chance to correct it, or a mechanism can allow review, confirmation and correction before finalizing. It does not prescribe a particular animation or require every routine save to open a modal.

This distinction helps a team select proportionate safeguards. Editing a draft title should not feel like authorizing a bank transfer. Conversely, changing a label from “Submit” to “Confirm” does not create a genuine opportunity to review an irreversible consequence.

Is hold-to-confirm enough?

No. A hold gesture can add deliberate friction and visible progress, as the example shows, but it cannot verify the amount, enforce permissions or prevent a duplicate server request. It can also be difficult for some people to operate. Preserve an appropriate accessible alternative, and test with keyboard and assistive-technology users.

The hold interaction here is a prototype to inspect, not a production payment control or a claim of accessibility certification. In a real system, the confirmation design sits alongside server-side validation, authorization, transaction integrity and audit requirements.

A four-part review for dangerous actions

  1. Name the outcome. Prefer “Delete this project” over an ambiguous “Continue”.
  2. Show the affected item. Include enough context to distinguish it from similar records.
  3. Offer correction. Let people return to change the information without losing everything.
  4. Handle the result. Explain success, failure or uncertainty without encouraging accidental retries.

How should a team test this?

Ask a participant to complete a specific task without explaining the controls. Observe what they believe will happen before they act. Test cancellation, slow responses, accidental double activation and interrupted sessions. Use synthetic accounts and data; do not experiment with real destructive operations.

When planning custom business software, agree these recovery paths alongside the happy path. The button-design checklist covers the smaller interaction details.
